What we believe

Fear is a lousy motivator.

Other firms scare you into compliance; we have no interest in that. We work with people who already care. Compliance is the floor, not the goal.

Accessibility is a craft.

Automated scans catch about a quarter of the problems. The rest takes hands on a screen reader and judgment you can't script. (A screen reader is the software people who are blind or have low vision use to hear a page read aloud.)

The fix matters more than the finding.

Anyone can hand you a list of issues. We pair with your engineers until the barriers are actually gone. Then we re-test.

Our philosophy

Access to the internet is a civil right.

We mean that literally. One of our founders taught user experience design in Haiti and co-founded a digital-literacy nonprofit there. Getting people online is one half of access. Digital accessibility is the other half. School, work, and public life happen online now, and a connection means little if what loads cannot be used.

That is why we don't ration attention. Every client gets as much of it as they need, for as long as they need it, and we work with them until they meet WCAG 2.2 (the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) and the regulations that apply to them.

Our tool

We built the software our audits run on.

Labrador is our guided WCAG conformance testing platform, and it is partly why our audits come back consistent. Testers work each success criterion in order, page by page and component by component, recording severity and remediation status as they go. Nothing depends on a spreadsheet someone forgot to update.

Evidence lands in the same record. Screenshots and video attach to the finding they document, and your report is generated from that record rather than retyped into it. Every export is built for the person receiving it. Reviewers get beautiful, interactive HTML reports with embedded media and clickable criterion links. Procurement gets a DOCX VPAT. Your developers get a Jira-ready CSV export, or a direct Jira integration that moves findings straight into their backlog. And anyone who just wants a spreadsheet gets a plain CSV.

It gives the manual work structure rather than replacing it, and it is a tool for our testers, never something that runs on your site.

On widgets and overlays

Accessibility isn't something you can install.

Widgets, often sold as overlays, promise compliance in a line of JavaScript. They sit on top of a site and patch it in the browser, but the barriers live in the code underneath, and that is where they stay. Often the widget adds new ones, intercepting keyboard focus or overriding the screen reader someone has already tuned to their liking.

The lawsuit data backs this up. UsableNet, which tracks ADA website lawsuits, finds that more than one in five lawsuits are now brought against sites that already had a widget running.

If you have one today, that is not a mark against you. Plenty of teams were sold one in good faith. Ask us and we will tell you plainly what it does and does not cover.
